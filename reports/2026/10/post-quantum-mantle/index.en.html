Post-Quantum from First Principles to Roadmap: What Ethereum, Starknet and Aztec Are Doing, and What Mantle Can Do Now

Quantum computers threaten signatures and proofs, not hashes. Ethereum's 2029 target covers only the L1 protocol. Mantle must migrate its own proof verifier, L1 multisig keys and L2 account rules, and it can start most of that work now.

Bottom line: a quantum computer can solve the elliptic-curve problem. Because of that, EVM signatures, BLS consensus, KZG commitments and BN254 SNARK proofs all fail. Hashes only lose some security margin (F1, F3). Ethereum aims to make all three layers of the L1 protocol quantum-resistant by December 2029. It does not migrate an L2's proof verifier, admin keys or accounts (F11, F12).

Today, Mantle's state security rests on two things: one PLONK verification on BN254, and a set of secp256k1 multisig signers. Neither is on Ethereum's roadmap (F2). So "wait for Ethereum to choose, then rebase" is not enough. The inventory, permission hardening, PQ account prototypes and proof-path design can start now (F15, F16).

Context

The problem: on 2026-10-15, Aztec published its PQ roadmap with two targets, 2027-02 and 2030 [23]. A few days earlier we reviewed Starknet's PQ migration [26]. The Ethereum Foundation (EF) aims to make all three layers of the L1 quantum-resistant by December 2029 [3]. Mantle is an EVM-equivalent L2 with no public PQ research or plan [35][36]. I

Background

B-0

Four crypto tools: signatures, encryption, hashes, proofs

~3min
What it is

A signature answers "did the owner authorize this". Encryption answers "who can read this". A hash answers "was the data changed". A proof answers "was the computation done by the rules". Each rests on different math, so quantum computers affect each one differently.

Why it matters here

Any PQ discussion must first name the tool. "Hashes are quantum-safe" does not mean "signatures are quantum-safe". "The proof system is a STARK" does not mean "the signatures are PQ".

Minimal example

Alice's withdrawal: ECDSA signature (signature) → blob KZG (commitment) → PLONK (proof) → keccak withdrawal proof (hash).

Common misconception

Myth: PQ means swapping one signature scheme. Fact: signatures, commitments, proofs and consensus each migrate separately, at different speeds [1][3].

Go deeper

[1] pq.ethereum.org FAQ

B-1

Elliptic curves and the discrete log (ECDLP)

~4min
What it is

Elliptic-curve security rests on one problem: given the public key P=kG, find the private key k. A classical computer needs about 2128 steps, the same security strength as AES-128 (P61).

Why it matters here

secp256k1 (EOAs), BLS12-381 (consensus, KZG) and BN254 (L2 proof verification) all rest on this problem. If someone solves it, they all fail together [2][17].

Minimal example

EOA address = keccak256(pubkey)[12:]. After one transaction, anyone can recover the public key from the signature and attack it offline [14].

Common misconception

Myth: the address is a hash of the public key, so it is safe. Fact: only addresses that never sent a transaction keep the extra hash layer [2].

Go deeper

[17] Babbush et al. 2026 §II

B-2

Shor's algorithm and Grover's algorithm

~4min
What it is

Shor solves discrete logs and integer factoring efficiently on a quantum computer; it changes the method, not just the speed. Grover takes the square root of the number of tries in an unstructured search, and it runs serially, so adding machines barely helps.

Why it matters here

This decides what to replace and what only needs new parameters. Mantle must replace anything Shor breaks. For what only Grover affects, NIST says AES-128 and SHA-256 stay usable [18].

Minimal example

For a 256-bit hash, the theoretical preimage queries drop from 2256 to 2128. Once NIST adds circuit-depth limits, a SHA-256 preimage is still in the top security category (Category 5) [18].

Common misconception

Myth: quantum computers break all cryptography. Fact: symmetric ciphers and hashes only lose margin [18].

Go deeper

[18] NIST IR 8547 ipd §2.1.3

B-3

Trusted setup and SNARK wrapping

~5min
What it is

The public parameters (SRS) of Groth16 / PLONK hide a secret number τ. Classically, if one participant in the ceremony destroys their share, nobody knows τ. Many zkVMs first make a large STARK proof, then "wrap" it in a small PLONK / Groth16 proof for L1.

Why it matters here

A quantum computer can compute τ directly from the public SRS. That gives a reusable backdoor to forge proofs [17]. Mantle's final proof is PLONK on BN254 [37].

Minimal example

SP1: RISC-V execution → STARK (hash-based) → PLONK wrapper (BN254, Aztec Ignition SRS) → L1 verification, ~300k gas [31].

Common misconception

Myth: one honest participant makes the setup quantum-safe. Fact: τ is the discrete log of points in the SRS, and Shor can solve it [17][41].

Go deeper

[17] Babbush et al. 2026 §V.5

B-4

PQ signature families

~5min
What it is

Post-quantum (PQ) cryptography runs on ordinary computers and aims to resist quantum computers. Signatures come in three main families: lattices (ML-DSA, Falcon), hashes (SLH-DSA, XMSS) and isogenies (SQIsign).

Why it matters here

The families differ in assumptions, sizes and implementation difficulty. On-chain, size and verification gas matter most.

Minimal example

Signature sizes: ECDSA 65 B; Falcon-512 666 B; ML-DSA-44 2,420 B; SLH-DSA-128s 7,856 B [20].

Common misconception

Myth: PQ needs quantum hardware. Fact: PQ algorithms run on ordinary CPUs [19].

Go deeper

[20] FIPS 204 Table 2

B-5

Hybrid signatures: must be AND

~l2
What it is

During a transition, use one classical signature and one PQ signature together. Accept only when both pass (AND). Then an attacker must break both.

Why it matters here

Many migration designs accidentally become "either one passes" (OR). OR is only as strong as the weaker scheme [38].

Go deeper

[38] IETF composite signatures draft -19 §9.1

B-6

HNDL: harvest now, decrypt later

~l2
What it is

An attacker stores ciphertext today and decrypts it once a quantum computer exists.

Why it matters here

It only hurts confidentiality. Signatures on a public chain need no HNDL defense; privacy apps and encrypted data do [1][23].

Go deeper

[23] Aztec blog, "Protecting private data now"

The cryptography an EVM chain uses today

An EVM chain relies on discrete-log assumptions in four places: account signatures, consensus signatures, DA commitments and L2 proof verification. Hashes appear only in the state tree and in withdrawal proofs [2][8][41].

F1

An EVM chain relies on discrete logs in four places, and Shor breaks all of them

confidence: high
Claim

Accounts (secp256k1 ECDSA, EIP-7702 authorizations, P-256), consensus (BLS12-381), DA (KZG) and L2 proof verification (BN254 precompiles) all rest on the elliptic-curve discrete log. The Keccak used in the state tree and withdrawal proofs only loses margin.

Evidence

observed EIP-7702, EIP-4844, EIP-197 and the consensus-specs text [8][41] (E1–E4, E6–E14); ethereum.org lists ECDSA, BLS, KZG and pairing-based ZK proofs as solvable by Shor [2] (T11); NIST IR 8105 Table 1 [18] (T1). inferred The original ECDSA key can bypass a PQ module on a 7702 account (E5).

Implication

"Is Mantle quantum-safe?" needs four separate answers, one per place. A single verdict cannot cover them.

EIP-7702 has a detail that is easy to miss. The protocol checks the authorization tuple with ecrecover. The same private key can re-delegate or clear the delegation at any time, and a delegated EOA can still send transactions directly [8]. So a PQ verification module on a 7702 account does not remove the original ECDSA root authority (E2–E5).

K

What happens when quantum computers arrive

Google sorts quantum attacks into three kinds [17]:

  • at-rest: attack public keys that stay on-chain for a long time, such as EOAs that have sent transactions and multisig signers. There is plenty of time to compute offline.
  • on-spend: compute the private key in the few minutes between broadcast and inclusion. This needs a quantum computer that finishes in minutes.
  • on-setup: attack fixed public parameters, such as the KZG and PLONK SRS. One computation yields a reusable backdoor.
F3

Quantum computers break signatures, commitments and proofs, not hashes

confidence: high
Claim

Shor directly breaks ECDSA, BLS, KZG, Groth16 / PLONK and ECDH. Grover only reduces the margin of hashes and AES. NIST says AES-128 "will remain secure for decades", and a SHA-256 preimage stays in Category 5.

Evidence

observed NIST IR 8105 Table 1, the PQC FAQ and IR 8547 ipd [18] (T1–T6); Google white paper §III.4 [17] (T9). inferred "A 256-bit hash drops to 2^128" counts queries only (T7).

Implication

Mantle does not need to replace keccak or the MPT. It must replace every signature and the proof wrapper, and it inherits BLS and KZG from Ethereum.

F4

A trusted setup is broken once and becomes a reusable backdoor

confidence: high
Claim

The KZG and PLONK trapdoor τ is the discrete log of elliptic-curve points in the public SRS. Once a quantum computer computes τ, an attacker can forge blob data proofs or SNARK proofs on an ordinary computer, with no further quantum computation.

Evidence

observed Google white paper §V.5, "a persistent, universal backdoor" [17] (T20); EF KZG ceremony FAQ: an extracted secret allows arbitrary claims about EIP-4844 data [41] (T21); Google concedes that its own SP1-generated Groth16 proof is not sound against quantum attacks [17] (T24); ethereum.org says the ceremony is secure "even against quantum computers" [2] (E51). inferred That ethereum.org claim is wrong (T23, E52).

Implication

Mantle's final proof is BN254 PLONK (Aztec Ignition SRS). After Q-day, an attacker could send a forged proof through an allowlisted proposer. L1 would then accept a wrong state root, and the attacker could drain the bridge. The proposer is itself an ECDSA EOA with an exposed public key (T25, K5, X3).

Google also quantifies the exposure on Ethereum [17]:

  • The 1,000 accounts holding the most ETH hold about 20.5M ETH. A fast-clock quantum computer could break them in less than 9 days (T15).
  • At least 70 of the top 500 contracts carry admin-key risk, about 2.5M ETH. Deriving those keys takes less than 15 hours (T16).
  • Bridges, oracles and other accounts that control much but hold little ETH carry a second-order risk that is "arguably much larger" (T17).
  • The paper also notes that moving Ethereum consensus to PQ does not retroactively fix quantum flaws in deployed contracts (T18).

Recent AI cryptanalysis and its risks

AI already changes cryptographic security at two levels:

  • Algorithms: it finds weaknesses in the math.
  • Implementations: it finds bugs in code and integrations.

F8

PQ signatures are 10–121× larger, and checking them without a precompile costs 200× more

confidence: high
Claim

An ML-DSA-44 signature is about 37× an ECDSA signature, and SLH-DSA-128s about 121×. Pure-contract verification costs at best about 650k gas (Falcon) to 1.23M gas (ML-DSA-44), about 217–409× ecrecover. Draft precompiles bring this to 3,000–4,500 gas, but no Ethereum fork includes them.

Evidence

observed size tables in FIPS 204 / 205 and the Falcon spec [20] (P24–P27); ZKNOX and Fireblocks READMEs [40] (P29–P31); EIP-8051 / 8052 [12] (P32, P33). inferred Ratio arithmetic (P28, P36).

Implication

If every transaction carries a PQ signature, the cost lands on calldata and on verification. High-frequency use cases (perps, agents) should consider session keys or batched authorization, but must not keep an unrestricted ECDSA back door.

Hybrid signatures must use AND. The IETF composite signature draft accepts only when every component signature passes. It classes the OR form as "migration flexibility" that does not give dual-algorithm security [38] (P38–P40). Web2 already uses hybrids at scale for key exchange: in 2026-09, about 70% of browser traffic used hybrid ML-KEM (X25519MLKEM768) [39] (P41).

Acceptance rule After an attacker breaks the classical scheme
AND: both the classical and the PQ signature must pass ✓the attacker must still break the PQ signature
OR: either signature passes ✗the attacker passes with the classical signature alone
F9

The key to hybrids and migration is AND, and Mantle's proof gateway is OR by design

confidence: high
Claim

A hybrid signature forces an attacker to break both algorithms only in AND mode. The SP1VerifierGateway that Mantle uses accepts any route that its owner added and did not freeze; routes have no AND relation.

Evidence

observed IETF draft -19 §3.3, §9.1 [38] (P38, P39); SP1VerifierGateway.verifyProof source [34] (K7). inferred Adding a PQ route without freezing the old BN254 route means "old proof OR new proof" (K9, X4).

Implication

When the proof system changes, freezing the old route must be a written migration step. Succinct has already frozen 11 old routes (K8), so the operation is proven.

PQ proof systems and PQ signatures are different things. Signatures protect "who authorized". Proof systems protect "was the computation correct" [1].

  • Hash-based proofs (STARK, FRI, WHIR) rely only on hashes; researchers consider them plausibly PQ. The cost is size: fast schemes produce proofs of about 300 KB [23] (P47).
  • Lattice SNARKs (Lattice Jolt / Akita, 2026-09) produce proofs under 100 KB, but do not yet have zero knowledge [33] (P51, P52).
  • Production zkVMs wrap their proofs in a BN254 SNARK before going on-chain, as the figure below shows.
F10

confidence: high

SP1 uses Gnark's BN254 Groth16 / PLONK to compress a STARK into a SNARK for on-chain verification. RISC Zero labels its own STARK layer "Quantum Safe: Yes" and its Groth16 layer "No". SP1 Hypercube's inner layer also assumes a hard discrete log on an extension-field elliptic curve.

Evidence

observed SP1 Security Model [31] (P54, P56, O35, O36); RISC Zero Security Model [42] (P57). inferred On-chain verification of "STARK inside + BN254 wrapper" is not quantum-resistant (P58, O37). No public analysis shows whether a quantum attacker can exploit the inner extension-field curve (P59, speculative).

Implication

To judge whether a rollup's proofs are PQ, look at what L1 verifies last. For Mantle, that is PLONK on BN254 (K2, K3).

F11

Ethereum has scheduled only EIP-8141, and calls PQ precompiles premature

confidence: high
Claim

Hegotá schedules only FOCIL and EIP-8141 (Frame Transaction); the EF says "Hegotá is not the PQ fork". 8141 has no PQ signature scheme of its own, only reserved extension slots. Hegotá declined the ML-DSA precompile EIP-8355 as "premature standardization", and 7885 / 8051 / 8052 are on no list. For consensus, leanVM calls itself "not (yet) production ready", and the hash function is not chosen.

Evidence

observed EIP-8081 Hegotá meta [5] (E22, E23, E27, E28); EF Hegotá tier list [4] (E34); EIP-8141 signature scheme table [7] (E25); leanVM README @127510d1 [13] (E43, E44); ethereum.org Hegotá page [2] (E24). inferred Ethereum's canonical hash function is not yet chosen (E48).

Implication

If Mantle wants to "follow Ethereum's algorithm choice", there is no signature algorithm to follow before 2027. On the account layer, the only thing to follow is the 8141 framework.

F12

L2s inherit only consensus and DA, and must migrate the rest themselves

confidence: high
Claim

The EF gives L2s advice, not protocol requirements. It names four things to protect: sequencer and admin keys, bridges and cross-chain messaging, AA accounts, and the zk-rollup proof system; it calls protocol evolution "chain-specific". L2s benefit automatically only from PQ L1 consensus and blob DA, and even then must switch blob transaction versions.

Evidence

observed pq.ethereum.org FAQ, "Ecosystem & Broader Context" [1] (E59); ethereum.org on application-layer ZK proofs [2] (E60); EIP-4844 rationale for versioned hashes [41] (E61); EIP-8151 on L2 fault proofs [9] (E62). inferred An L2's BN254 verifier, account rules, bridge and upgrade keys do not become PQ on their own (E63–E65).

Implication

"Rebasing on Ethereum" gives Mantle only the inherited layer. Execution-layer changes such as 8141 / 8151 also need a Mantle hard fork to activate.

Emergency plan. In 2024-03, Vitalik proposed a "quantum emergency hard fork". It is a research post with no EIP [14] (E56). It has four steps:

  1. roll back to before the mass theft;
  2. disable EOA transactions;
  3. add smart-wallet transactions;
  4. users prove with a STARK that they know the seed behind their key, then switch to new validation code.

Starknet: no new progress since our 10-14 report. The Migration Register is still the 2026-08-19 version [26] (O1–O3). Starknet's edge comes from two design choices: a hash-based proving layer, and native accounts whose signature scheme the protocol does not fix. But the official answer to "Is Starknet quantum-resistant?" is "No. Not as a single system-wide claim." [26].

Aztec (2026-10-15, by Zac Williamson and Mike Connor) [23]:

  • Two targets: hardened libraries and dev patterns against HNDL by 2027-02; full migration by 2030 (O5).
  • Three selection milestones: signatures 2027-03, public-key encryption 2027-07, proof system 2027-11 (O6).
  • The hard part of the proof system is recursion. Fast PQ proofs are about 300 KB, against Aztec's current ~20 KB. 2-to-1 aggregation must take about 20–100 ms on a modern CPU to be practical (O8, O9).
  • Addresses: move the keys baked into the address to a keystore registry, so the address becomes a plain hash (O10).
  • Hashes: replace Poseidon2 with BLAKE2s / BLAKE3 in the long run, likely following the EF's choice (O11).
  • The authors think Q-day within 4 years is unlikely (O12).

Optimism (2026-01-14, Karl Floersch) [25]:

  • OP Labs commits to retire ECDSA EOAs within 10 years, subject to governance approval. By 2036-01, every EOA must delegate key management to a PQ smart account (O18).
  • The sequencer and batch submitter move to PQ signatures; the signature scheme is not chosen (O19, O20).
  • The post does not mention the proposer, fault proofs or L1 contract upgrade permissions (O21).
  • Mantle is not in the superchain-registry, so this roadmap does not bind Mantle automatically (M37, M40).

Algorand's migration semantics are a useful contrast. After a user rekeys to a PQ address, the address stays the same and "only the Falcon key can authorize"; the old key loses its power [27] (O26). EIP-7702 works the other way: after delegation, the old ECDSA key keeps its authority. That is why Optimism needs a protocol-level retirement of ECDSA transactions in 2036 as a backstop (O53).

Mantle's state security rests on one BN254 proof and a set of ECDSA multisigs

confidence: high
Claim

The latest state submission on 2026-10-15 carried a 964-byte proof routed to SP1 v6.1.0's PLONK verifier, which relies on BN254 pairings. Not just waiting: what Mantle can do

These are recommendations based on the evidence above. Mantle has not reviewed them internally.

F15

Mantle can move before Ethereum, and it has done so before

confidence: medium
Claim

Mantle enabled a P-256 precompile through RIP-7212 before L1 shipped EIP-7951. It uses its own named hard forks (Skadi, Limb, Arsia) to follow or get ahead of EVM features. Safe multisigs also accept EIP-1271 contract signers, which can check PQ signatures in a contract without waiting for L1 AA.

Evidence

observed the Limb changelog and reads at Mantle blocks from 2025-05 and 2025-10 show a P-256 precompile before Fusaka (2025-12-03) [36][37] (M33, K22, E7); Safe contract signatures, v=0 [37] (K21); Fireblocks ML-DSA-44 verification at ~1.23M gas [40] (P31). inferred PQ precompiles can ship early, but FIPS 206 is not final, so a Falcon interface risks rework (X5, X6); Mantle can replace multisig signers with PQ signers step by step (X19).

Implication

The PQ signer contracts must have no ECDSA authorization or upgrade bypass, or an attacker can skip the PQ check (X19). 7702 accounts are the exception. A PQ module does not remove the ECDSA root authority; Mantle needs a protocol-level k1 retirement similar to EIP-8151 (X7).

F16

Making the proof path PQ needs Succinct, and the old route must be frozen

confidence: medium
Claim

One state submission costs about 431k gas today. Verifying a PQ STARK directly on L1 costs on the order of 10M gas, and the calldata alone is about 4.9M gas. Succinct's public direction is to replace Groth16 with a hash-based wrapper (VEIL), with no date. Succinct's 2/3 Safe manages the Gateway, and adding or freezing routes does not go through Mantle.

Evidence

observed On-chain gasUsed [37] (K4); Vitalik's gas magnitudes [15] (E53); VEIL blog [32] (O38); Gateway owner and source [34][37] (K6, K7). inferred Calldata cost of a ~300 KiB proof (P49); a Succinct hash-based wrapper and L1-side aggregation (such as EIP-8288) must move together (X8, X14).

Implication

This is the hardest item for Mantle to finish alone. What it can do now is write the requirements, acceptance criteria and route-freeze procedure into its partnership plan with Succinct.

Six workstreams (the first three depend on no upstream party):

  1. Publish a cryptography dependency inventory. Follow the Starknet Migration Register format. List five fields per component: algorithm, key holder, the permission that can change it, PQ status, upstream dependencies (X9). Start with the 9 items in the panel P table.
  2. Tighten permissions. Add an upgrade timelock to the ProxyAdmin. Assess moving the batcher and proposer to multisigs or restricted roles. Replace Safe signers step by step with EIP-1271 PQ signer contracts. These contracts must have no ECDSA bypass (X12, X19). These steps also cut today's classical risk; L2BEAT already rates upgrades with no delay as CRITICAL (M18).
  3. Prototype PQ accounts. Build an ML-DSA smart account on Mantle with ERC-4337 / ERC-1271. Following Algorand's semantics, test whether a leaked old ECDSA key can bypass the new account. Also assess shipping PQ precompiles and a k1 retirement in a Mantle hard fork (X6, X7).
  4. Design the proof path with Succinct. Write four acceptance criteria (X4, X8, X14):
    • what L1 verifies last does not rely on elliptic curves;
    • dual proofs during the transition use AND;
    • Succinct freezes the old route after the switch;
    • the plan includes L1 gas and latency estimates.
  5. Follow L1. Track Hegotá (8141), K* (PQ attestations) and leanDA. Prepare the switch of blob transaction versions and the activation of 8141 / 8151 on Mantle (E61, E65).
  6. Emergency plan and audits. Write a Q-day plan: who can pause, who can change the verifier, and do those permissions rely on ECDSA. Require AI-assisted audits and formal verification for every new cryptographic component (X13, X18).

The phases above are a speculative recommendation (X15, [speculative]). The dates depend on three things: Succinct's progress, the FIPS 206 release date, and Ethereum's fork cadence. The window itself is uncertain. Suppose Ethereum finishes L1 PQ in 2029-12 and a CRQC arrives in the early-to-mid 2030s. Then an L2 that migrates only after L1 has just a few years left (X11, [speculative]).

In public statements, give PQ status per component and with a date, for example "Mantle supports ML-DSA smart accounts (testnet)". Until the proof path, L1 permissions and account root authority have all migrated, do not use blanket claims such as "Mantle is quantum-resistant" or "PQ-ready".
