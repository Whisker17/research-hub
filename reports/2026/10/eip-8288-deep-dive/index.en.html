EIP-8288 Deep Dive: Recursive STARK Mempools, an AggLayer Comparison, and the Recursion Security Debate

8288 turns signature checks into block-level "dependencies" that the mempool aggregates recursively. AggLayer shares only the "proof verifies a proof" primitive. Recursion has a theory gap but no known attack.

The conclusions first, in four points:

  1. EIP-8288 is not a new signature scheme. It adds a dependency frame to frame transactions. The frame declares claims and runs no code. Signatures stay off-chain, mempool nodes aggregate them recursively every second, and the block carries one STARK (F1). The current draft accepts only one scheme, leanSPHINCS (F4).
  2. Supporters say "more nodes means more capacity". The spec has no work split and no incentives. It also caps a mode 1 wrapper at 16 signatures, which conflicts with "one wrapper covers every active transaction" (F6).
  3. AggLayer is similar in one way only: it verifies one SP1 proof inside another. But it is a single fixed layer, it settles chain by chain, and it posts PLONK/BN254 proofs to L1. It is not multi-party recursion (F9, F10).
  4. The literature supports Thaler's critique of recursion. Recursion has no known security proof in the plain random oracle model. 8288 itself lists it as a trust assumption (F11). But the practical Fiat–Shamir attacks reviewed here need a circuit or program the attacker chooses. The current draft does not allow that. We found no exploitable flaw (F12, F13).

Conclusion

8288's value is making verification dependencies explicit and letting the network share the proving. Its risk sits in one unproven recursion assumption and an unfinished draft.
  • Mechanism: dependency frames declare and do not execute; the mempool aggregates recursively every second; the block verifies one STARK; the account decides whether a dependency is its authorization (F1, F2).
  • Status: leanSPHINCS only; AGGREGATED_VK, DEPS_HASH and the reference implementation are TBD; not in Hegotá (F3, F4).
  • Supporters' claims: "validators verify one proof" holds; "constant proof bandwidth" holds for the proof part only; "more nodes, more capacity" lacks a work split, incentives and throughput data (F5, F6, F7).
  • AggLayer: shares only the primitive of verifying an SP1 proof inside another; settles chain by chain, submitted by a trusted aggregator, not PQ (F9, F10).
  • Recursion: the theory gap is real and both sides' texts admit it; known attacks need a chosen circuit; no exploitable flaw in 8288 found; Aerie also relies on the random oracle model (F11–F14).

Context

The problem. PQ signatures are about 2–3 kB and cost about 150,000–200,000 gas to verify on-chain. Client-side STARKs are 128–512 kB [1] (spec Motivation). If every tx puts this data in the block and every node verifies it one by one, PQ and private txs both become expensive.

Why now.

  • On 2026-09-09, Vitalik posted about EIP-8288 and said he hopes it ships in I-star, the fork after Hegotá [6] (C2). decentrek then posted twice about it, calling it "my new favorite EIP of all time" [8][9] (C3).

Background

B-1

Frame transactions (EIP-8141)

~l2
What it is

A frame transaction splits one tx into several frames, each with a mode. A VERIFY frame validates and approves payment; a SENDER frame executes as the sender. The account writes its own validation logic, so a new signature scheme needs no hard fork. Contracts read other frames of the same tx with FRAMEPARAM / FRAMEDATACOPY.

Why it matters here

8288 adds one new frame mode, mode 4, to 8141. Hegotá includes 8141 but not 8288 [3][5] (E26).

Common misconception

Myth: 8141 ships PQ signatures. Fact: 8141 gives the framework; the account picks the scheme.

[3] EIP-8141

B-2

STARKs and recursive proofs

~4min
What it is

A STARK is a proof system that relies only on hashes and needs no trusted setup. Recursion means one proof verifies one or more other proofs. Many proofs can then merge, layer by layer, into one proof of about the same size.

Why it matters here

All of 8288's scaling comes from recursion: a mempool node takes other nodes' proofs as input and proves that they are all valid [1] (E13).

Minimal example

leanVM 2→1 recursion merges two child proofs into one in 0.291 s; the output is 201.5 KiB and verifies in about 6 ms [14] (C11).

Common misconception

Myth: recursion just bundles files. Fact: the outer circuit runs the inner verifier, including the inner hash computations.

Go deeper

[14] leanVM README, "recursion" section

B-3

The Fiat–Shamir transform and the random oracle model (ROM)

~5min
What it is

In an interactive proof, the verifier sends random challenges. Fiat–Shamir replaces each challenge with a hash of the transcript so far, so the prover can send the proof offline. Security proofs usually treat that hash as an ideal random black box. This is the random oracle model (ROM).

Why it matters here

leanVM claims ROM/QROM soundness; Aerie proves knowledge soundness in the classical ROM [14][17] (R9, R10). In the ROM, parties can query the hash oracle, but an ordinary circuit has no concrete implementation of it to evaluate. A recursive circuit must compute the inner hash, and Thaler's critique starts here [10].

Minimal example

The challenge is c=H(statement,transcript). The ROM assumes the output of H is fully random; real systems use BLAKE2s or Poseidon2.

Common misconception

Myth: a ROM proof is a real-world proof. Fact: researchers built contrived schemes that are secure in the ROM and insecure with any concrete hash [24] (R16).

Go deeper

[27] ePrint 2024/728 §1

B-4

Knowledge soundness and extractors

~5min
What it is

Soundness only says a false statement cannot pass. Knowledge soundness asks for more: if a prover can make the verifier accept, an "extractor" exists. The extractor can pull the witness, such as the signature itself, out of that prover.

Why it matters here

Signature aggregation needs knowledge soundness. "A valid signature exists for this key and message" is often simply true; what matters is that the prover has the signature [17]. Under recursion, the extractor must dig down layer by layer, and the cost adds up [25] (R28).

Minimal example

A standard extractor rewinds the prover and reruns it, then combines answers to different challenges into a witness. Each extra recursion layer means rewinding the inner layer again.

Common misconception

Myth: a costly extractor means an attack. Fact: it is a loss in the proof technique, not an attack success rate (R29).

Go deeper

[26] ePrint 2023/1646 abstract

B-5

IVC and PCD

~l2
What it is

IVC (incrementally verifiable computation) is a chain: each step proves that the previous step was correct and so is this one. PCD (proof-carrying data) extends this to trees and graphs: parties who do not trust each other each produce proofs, and the proofs merge.

Why it matters here

The 8288 mempool aggregation tree has the shape of PCD. The "depth" in the recursion-security papers is the height of this tree (R6).

[26] ePrint 2023/1646

How EIP-8288 works

What it is. The title of EIP-8288 is "In-mempool signature and proof aggregation". Its authors are Vitalik Buterin and Thomas Coratger, and its status is Draft [1] (E1). It was first merged on 2026-09-09 and had 10 commits by 10-15 [2] (E2).

Fig 1 compares "verify each signature in the EVM" with the 8288 approach.

What Alice's tx looks like. Each claim in a dependency frame is a 96-byte triple (scheme, data_hash, verification_key_hash), with 1–256 triples per frame [1] (E4). For leanSPHINCS, the third field holds the 32-byte public key directly [1] (E5).

An all-zero data_hash means "this tx's signature hash". A tx cannot write its own signature hash into a frame, because that hash covers the frame data. Dependency extraction swaps in the signature hash for proving. The frame bytes do not change, and FRAMEDATACOPY still returns zeros, so the VERIFY frame checks for zero bytes. The spec added this rule on 10-15 [1][12] (E8, E9).

Fig 2 shows the full path of this tx from wallet to block.

What the recursive circuit does. Below is the spec's own pseudocode. It does four things: verify direct dependencies; verify child proofs with the same AGGREGATED_VK and merge their dependencies; remove discards; deduplicate and compare with the public input [1] (E13).

The circuit does not understand txs or blocks; it sees only a flat list of triples [1] (E13). The spec says it passes AGGREGATED_VK into the proof "to enable unlimited-depth recursion" [1] (E14).

How nodes verify a block. The block header gains a field recursive_stark = [stark_proof, block_deps_hash]. The proof carries no dependency list. Validating nodes recompute block_deps_hash from the block's txs and verify against the fixed AGGREGATED_VK [1] (E11). So a builder cannot include Alice's tx and quietly drop her dependency.

F1

8288 makes signature checks a block validity condition, not a call inside the EVM

confidence: high
Claim

A dependency frame (mode 4) runs no EVM code; it only declares triples that must hold. For a block to be valid, the recursive STARK in its header must prove every dependency of the block (after deduplication). Mempool nodes, FOCIL creators and builders do "exactly the same task": verify inputs, merge, discard, prove again.

Evidence

observed EIP-8288 sections on dependency frames, block validity and the builder [1] (E3, E11, E13, E21).

Implication

Dependencies are visible before EVM execution. The spec's reason: the mempool does not need to run contract code to find a missing proof, and a revert cannot make a dependency disappear.

F2

The proof says only "this key signed this hash"; the account decides on authorization

confidence: high
Claim

The aggregation circuit only proves that the triples hold. The account must read the dependencies in its VERIFY frame with FRAMEPARAM / FRAMEDATACOPY and compare the key and data_hash itself. The spec's Test Case 1 does exactly this.

Evidence

observed the spec's execution-visibility section and Test Case 1 [1] (E9). inferred binding to authorization is the account code's job (E10).

Implication

Account authors cannot skip this step. An attacker's valid signature on "hello" also passes aggregation, but must not give the attacker Alice's rights.

F3

One leanSPHINCS dependency costs about 33,000 gas, a flat charge per claim, paid even on revert

confidence: high
Claim

The dependency frame's execution gas is 3,000 per leanSPHINCS claim or 30,000 per leanSTARK claim. Each claim, duplicates included, also adds 30,000 to intrinsic gas. One leanSPHINCS claim therefore costs about 33,000 gas, before the fee for its 96 B of data. The first draft added this charge to the block's base fee by the block's total dependency count; on 10-07 the tx became the payer.

Evidence

observed the Gas Accounting section and commit 9ee171b [1][2] (E23, E25). inferred the 33,000 total and the flat, not block-shared, charge (E24).

Implication

The spec calls this an "expected cost", not a measurement. "3,000 gas per signature" leaves out most of the charge.

F4

The current draft accepts only leanSPHINCS; key constants and the reference implementation are TBD

confidence: high
Claim

Every dependency's scheme must be LEANSPHINCS_SCHEME (0x10). The spec reserves LEANSTARK_SCHEME (0x11): a frame that uses it stays invalid until the spec defines get_deps_hash for it. AGGREGATED_VK, DEPS_HASH and the reference implementation all read TBD. 8288 is not in any Hegotá list.

Evidence

observed constants table, validity constraints and Reference Implementation [1] (E6, E15); EIP-8081 [5] (E26).

Implication

Today 8288 can natively aggregate only one hash-based signature scheme. The "any signature or proof" half has no spec yet.

L

Mempool recursion: do the supporters' claims hold?

What each node does. Every 1,000 ms, a node builds one mode 1 wrapper that covers every still-valid tx and forwards it to peers; txs it already sent travel as hashes only [1] (E18). When proving time runs short, the node uses this order [1]:

  1. its own aggregate from the previous round;
  2. items it must remove;
  3. other nodes' aggregates (by the number of new txs they add);
  4. user txs (by priority fee).

Fig 3 draws one aggregation tree for Alice, Bob and Charlie.

decentrek's five claims, premise by premise. decentrek argues that with 8288, more nodes means more signatures processed, while the network stays decentralized [8][9] (E39).

F5

"Constant proof bandwidth" holds only for the proof itself

confidence: medium
Claim

The spec says recursive STARK bandwidth is "constant per peer, regardless of the number of transactions", without a derivation. Tx bodies still go across the network once. Each wrapper carries the deduplicated dependency list (96 B per entry) and a list of tx hashes. The first hop, in mode 0, also carries raw signatures or STARKs.

Evidence

observed the spec's Censorship Resistance section and wrapper encoding [1] (E17, E18); Vitalik's post: "plus each transaction getting broadcasted through the network once" [6] (E29). inferred the parts that grow linearly with tx count (E40).

Implication

Count bandwidth in two parts: the proof is constant; txs and dependency lists are not.

F6

"More nodes, more capacity" lacks a work split, incentives and throughput data, and the mode 1 cap conflicts with it

confidence: high
Claim

The spec has every node aggregate the whole active pool, with no split of work across nodes and no proving incentive anywhere. It caps a mode 1 wrapper at ≤ 16 leanSPHINCS and ≤ 1 leanSTARK dependencies. It also requires "exactly one wrapper containing all currently active transactions" per round. Read literally, the two rules conflict. EIP-8292's consensus-layer benchmark aggregates 1,000 signatures in about 11 / 3.7 / 2.6 s on 2 / 8 / 16 cores. All three exceed the 1,000 ms interval.

Evidence

observed spec L251, L259, L263 and full text [1] (E18, E19); EIP-8292 benchmark [4] (E42). inferred the conflict and the missing mechanism (E20, E41). speculative "tens of thousands of PQ and private txs per second" has no primary source (E43).

Implication

The 16 cap may be a typo or may mean something else; neither the spec nor the forum thread says. Until the authors clarify it, the 8288 draft cannot support a "mempool scaling" argument.

F7

The block proof shows "included dependencies are valid", not "valid txs that arrived were included"

confidence: medium
Claim

In the Ethereum Magicians thread, several participants point out that the aggregate proof shows the included dependencies are valid. It does not show that the builder left out no valid dependency it received. For this, 8288 changes the FOCIL data structure so inclusion lists also carry a recursive proof, and attesters must verify it. FOCIL is already scheduled for Hegotá, so 8288 would change a live format.

Evidence

observed Magicians posts 3, 8 and 11 [11] (E37); the spec's FOCIL section [1] (E22); EIP-8081 [5] (E26).

Implication

The proof gives correctness. Censorship resistance still depends on inclusion-list rules. Judge the two separately.

The ISA debate. An ethresear.ch post of 10-08 argues that identifying a user's proof by vk_hash is only a placeholder. The proof system's VK changes whenever leanVM upgrades, which would break non-upgradable privacy pools. It proposes replacing 0x11 with a program scheme whose third field is program_id = hash(ISA version, program bytes) [12] (E38). The author also says there is not yet enough data to decide.

F8

"Wrap any signature or proof client-side in a STARK" does not work in the current draft

confidence: high
Claim

This path needs leanSTARK dependencies, which the spec reserves. The VK is only a hash with no registry, and nobody has chosen an ISA. A third-party experiment (ERC-8403) reports that leanVM's recursion guest today verifies only raw signatures and its own proofs, not proofs of other programs. We found no primary benchmark for proving Falcon, ML-DSA or privacy circuits with leanVM on a phone.

Evidence

observed the spec [1] (E6); the ISA post [12] (E38); the ERC-8403 post [33] (R43). inferred not usable today (E33). speculative client-side proving cost (E35).

Implication

Vitalik's "never need to add more precompiles again" is a direction, not today's state. Today only leanSPHINCS gets native aggregation.

AggLayer: what is similar, what is not

What AggLayer is. AggLayer connects many chains through one unified bridge. Its core component is the pessimistic proof. It assumes any connected chain can fail. So for each cross-chain state update, it proves that the chain took out no more than its balance in the bridge [20] (G13, G14). Pessimistic proofs have run on mainnet since 2025-02-03 [21] (G36).

Fig 4 shows how one certificate gets from a chain to L1.

F9

The similarity is real: the PP program verifies another SP1 proof inside SP1

confidence: high
Claim

AggchainProof::verify_aggchain_proof first assembles public values (previous and new local exit roots, L1 info root, network ID, aggchain params, and a commitment to imported bridge exits). It then calls sp1_zkvm::lib::verify::verify_sp1_proof(&self.aggchain_vkey, &public_values.hash()). The outer proof treats verifying the inner proof as part of its own computation.

Evidence

observed agglayer/crates/pessimistic-proof-core/src/aggchain_data/aggchain_proof.rs:L21-38@9f8e6c4 [18] (G1, G2); SP1 Proof Aggregation docs [22].

Implication

"AggLayer also uses recursion" is true at the primitive level. But the application fixes this one layer of composition, and the aggchain proof appears only in the "multisig + aggchain proof" mode (G3, G4).

F10

The differences are larger: per-certificate settlement, a trusted aggregator, not PQ end to end

confidence: high
Claim

The node builds one verifyPessimisticTrustedAggregator(rollupID, …) tx per certificate; the source has no path that merges several chains' certificates into one L1 proof. Only TRUSTED_AGGREGATOR_ROLE can call that function. The on-chain proof is SP1's PLONK wrap over BN254, and chain authentication uses secp256k1 ECDSA. SP1 Hypercube's memory argument also relies on an elliptic-curve discrete log assumption.

Evidence

observed certificate_task.rs:L379-446@9f8e6c4 [18] (G15); AgglayerManager.sol [19] (G18, G21); SP1 Security Model [23] (G22, G25). inferred no single cross-chain proof (G16); liveness and censorship resistance depend on the trusted aggregator (G19); not PQ end to end (G26).

Implication

AggLayer proves cross-chain ledger constraints, not tx signature aggregation. It neither shows that 8288 is safe nor is it a PQ design.

O

The recursion security debate

Who is critiquing. Justin Thaler is a Research Partner at a16z crypto research and an associate professor at Georgetown. He is also a co-author of the Aerie paper [10][17] (R1, R2). He is critiquing the approach that competes with his own, and readers should know this. His X handle @SuccinctJT is not related to Succinct, the company behind SP1 (C7).

His core sentence: "hash-only is not really hash-only: it rests on hashes plus the soundness of deep SNARK recursion" [10] (C4). In the same post he calls block-level aggregation "a powerful design independent of the hash-only vs. lattice debate". He does not oppose it [10] (C5).

Of the three authors of the KRS attack paper, two list Ethereum Foundation and one lists Succinct [28] (R20). Framing the debate as "Ethereum ignores the problem and a16z exposes it" does not match who did the research.

F11

The theory gap is real, and texts on the Ethereum side admit it

confidence: high
Claim

ePrint 2024/728 proves that relativized succinct arguments do not exist in the plain ROM. These are arguments that can prove computations which query the oracle. Building IVC/PCD that way is therefore ruled out. BCCT13 shows that standard proofs based on recursive extraction hold for a constant number of compositions only. 8288 lists "Soundness of recursive STARKs" as a trust assumption and says it "adds difficulty to end-to-end formally proving". leanerVM, the leanVM formal-verification project, lists recursive extraction (T6) as an open research question and notes that leanVM has no decreasing recursion-depth measure.

Evidence

observed ePrint 2024/728 abstract and §1 [27] (R25); BCCT13 [25] (R28); EIP-8288 Trust Assumptions [1] (R3, R4); leanerVM docs @28bbeb9 [15] (R34, R35). inferred both sides admit the gap (R37).

Implication

"Hash-only" does leave out one assumption: the soundness of recursive Fiat–Shamir proofs. Today that assumption is "widely believed", not proven.

F12

Thaler's wording is stronger than the theorem: "cannot be established" should read "no proof today, and the most natural route is ruled out"

confidence: high
Claim

The authors of 2024/728 write that ruling out standard (non-relativized) IVC and PCD in the ROM remains open. On the other side, 2023/1646 proves that if the base SNARK has straightline knowledge soundness, PCD is about as secure as the base. That holds only in an idealized oracle model, and the authors call it "heuristic, partial" evidence for recursive STARK parameters. 2025/1663 proves security at polynomial depth in the osROM, but it analyzes HyperNova, which uses elliptic curves.

Evidence

observed 2024/728 §1 [27] (R26); 2023/1646 abstract [26] (R30, R31); 2025/1663 abstract [29] (R32, R33). inferred Thaler's claim is stronger than the theorem (R27).

Implication

The recursion side has theoretical answers, but all of them need idealization beyond the ROM. A loose reduction does not mean an easy attack, and "no attack found" does not replace a reduction.

F13

Known attacks need the attacker to choose the circuit; the current draft does not allow that, but opening leanSTARK changes this

confidence: high
Claim

KRS builds, for a practical GKR-style argument and any concrete hash, a circuit that proves a false statement. The key is that the circuit computes its own Fiat–Shamir challenge. Fenzi extends the attack to Spartan and Aurora variants and gives a mitigation. None of these attacks targets FRI-style STARKs or recursion itself. Today the recursion tree holds only the protocol's fixed circuit. The spec reserves an entry for user-supplied VKs and admits they may recurse without a depth bound.

Evidence

observed ePrint 2025/118 [28] (R17, R19, R20); 2026/1838 [30] (R18, R21, R22); EIP-8288 [1] (R7, E14); Thaler: "we don't know of any outright attacks on SNARK recursion" [10] (R38). inferred the attacks need a chosen circuit; attackers cannot choose programs in the current draft; no exploitable flaw found (R8, R24, R39).

Implication

Once LEANSTARK_SCHEME opens, one concrete question needs an answer: can an attacker submit a self-referential program that computes leanVM's Fiat–Shamir hash inside itself? We found no public analysis that answers this question.

F14

Aerie also relies on the random oracle model; the real difference is whether the circuit must prove a hash

confidence: medium
Claim

Aerie proves knowledge soundness in the classical ROM and also uses Fiat–Shamir. The verifier computes HashToPoint outside the proof, so the proved computation does not evaluate HashToPoint and does not recurse. The common-salt mode needs an extra assumption with no known reduction to a standard lattice assumption. Aerie is a research release, and neither parameter set passes its own production criteria. leanVM also says it is "not (yet) production ready".

Evidence

observed Aerie paper abstract and README [17] (R10, R11, R14, R15); leanVM README [14] (R9). inferred the difference is whether the circuit proves the FS hash (R12, C9).

Implication

The first half of Thaler's point B ("all PQ SNARKs rely on the ROM") also covers Aerie. The debate centers on one assumption, recursion, not on whether hashes or lattices are safer.

F15

In practice, recursive aggregation has already shipped implementation bugs

confidence: medium
Claim

ZisK v1.3.2-alpha fixed a recursive-aggregation flaw. A prover could mark every child proof as null and skip the STARK checks. A final proof was then accepted for execution that was never proven. In SP1 before v3.0.0, the recursion circuit did not add the permutation argument's cumulative sum to the transcript before sampling a challenge. The advisory calls exploiting it "practically infeasible".

Evidence

observed ZisK release notes GHSA-3j5v-j2rm-9c5f [32] (R40); SP1 GHSA-8m24-3cfx-9fjw (GitHub Advisory) (R48). inferred these are implementation errors, not attacks on recursion theory (R41).

Implication

Recursion risk is not only theoretical. Recursive circuits, empty child proofs and challenge binding are all audit priorities.

Open points. Matthew Green raises a possibility in his post on KRS [35]. In a multi-program recursion tree, one malicious program's "bugdoor" might cross program boundaries and break the whole tree. This is a risk category with no known construction (R47, speculative). As of 10-21, we found no substantive public response to Thaler's post from EF, StarkWare or leanEthereum researchers (C10, speculative).

P

How to evaluate designs like this

This checklist follows from the research. It is not advice to any project.

Six questions for any recursive-aggregation design
  1. Is recursion depth bounded? 8288: no. leanVM has no decreasing depth measure (R6, R35).
  2. Can an attacker put a chosen program into the recursion tree? 8288 today: no. After leanSTARK opens: yes (R8, F13).
  3. Under which model are the security bits computed? ROM or QROM, one layer or with recursion (E44, E45, R9).
  4. Is the final verification path quantum-resistant end to end? An inner STARK with an outer BN254 wrap is not (G26).
  5. Who pays for proving, and who can censor? 8288 has no proving incentive; in AggLayer, a trusted aggregator submits settlements (E41, G19).
  6. Does it prove "everything included is valid", or also "nothing valid was left out"? (E37)

On the two positions (inferred).

  • The 8288 side: making dependencies explicit and sharing the proving across the network is a design contribution that stands apart from "hash vs lattice"; even the critic accepts it (C8). But "hash-only" needs the addition "plus the soundness of recursive Fiat–Shamir proofs" (R37).
  • The a16z side: the theory gap it points to is real (F11). But "cannot be established" overstates the theorem, and there is no concrete attack on 8288 yet (F12, F13). Aerie also relies on the ROM and is not production-ready either (F14).
  • Both sides call for cryptographic agility: Thaler argues for hybrid signatures and defense in depth, and says his team builds both lattice-Jolt and hash-Jolt (C6).
Q

Limits and open questions

  • No reproduction: all benchmarks are self-reported by their authors; we ran none.
  • Papers checked at abstract level: ePrint returned 403 for full-text fetches from our machine. We checked paper claims against abstracts and short PDF excerpts only, not theorem by theorem.
  • The mode 1 cap of 16: a typo or intended? Neither the spec nor the forum thread says (E19, E20).
  • Per-node throughput: we found no data on how many leanSPHINCS signatures normal hardware proves per 1,000 ms. We also found no cost curve for a builder that merges N child proofs.
  • Conflicting security numbers: the leanVM README says 128 / 64 bits; EIP-8292 says about 124 / 62 bits (E45).
  • Recursion under QROM: the README does not say whether leanVM's 64-bit QROM figure is for one layer or includes recursion.
  • AggLayer mainnet roles: we did not check on-chain who holds the trusted aggregator and upgrade roles, or whether a timelock applies.
  • Source of "AggLayer does something similar": not found (G42).
  • Responses: as of 10-21 we found no substantive response to Thaler's post (C10).
