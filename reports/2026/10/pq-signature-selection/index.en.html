Choosing Post-Quantum Signatures: Hash-Based vs Lattice-Based, and the \"Third Path\" of EIP-8288

The debate is really three choices — the signature scheme, the aggregation proof, and the protocol interface. Ethereum bets on hashes at the protocol layer and a16z bets on lattices; neither aggregation stack is production-ready. EIP-8288 wants the protocol to stop adding a precompile per algorithm, but the current draft supports only one hash-based signature.

Bottom line: the debate is often framed as "Ethereum picks hashes, a16z picks lattices". A more precise framing is that it argues three things at once (F1):

  1. which signature scheme to use;
  2. which proof system aggregates many signatures;
  3. how many kinds of cryptography the protocol should support natively.

Both sides have real weaknesses. The hash path rests trust on the oldest and best-studied primitive. But its signatures are large, proving hashes is expensive, and it needs recursion to split work across machines (F3, F4). The lattice path (Falcon + Aerie) proves 32,768 signatures in 0.65 s on one laptop. But it depends on younger lattice assumptions, and the FN-DSA standard has no draft yet (F6, F7). Both aggregation implementations say they are not production-ready (F8).

EIP-8288 tries to avoid picking a side. An account signs with any scheme locally, wraps it in a STARK, and a block-level recursive proof delivers the result. Vitalik says that this way "Ethereum can then never add a new precompile again". But the current draft accepts only one signature type, leanSPHINCS, and the generic STARK entry is not defined yet (F11, F12).

For Mantle: part 2's "contract account + ML-DSA verification precompile" still holds as near-term disaster recovery (F14). Four things change:

  • treat the precompile as a transitional tool;
  • fix the address range;
  • update the native AA timeline;
  • make accounts target a "verification backend", not one precompile address (F15).

Conclusion

Do not turn an algorithm choice into an architecture lock-in: ship PQ authorization now, target a verification backend, and wait on aggregation
  • Separate the layers: signature, aggregation proof and protocol interface are independent choices; Starknet already combines "hash-based proofs + Falcon accounts" (F1, F10).
  • Hash path: the most conservative assumption; the cost is large signatures, expensive proving, recursion, and the criticism that recursive security is hard to prove (F3, F4, F5).
  • Lattice path: Aerie proves 32,768 Falcon signatures in 0.65 s on one machine; the cost is younger assumptions, salts that make the default size non-constant, and no FN-DSA draft (F6, F7).
  • Neither is ready: leanVM and Aerie both say they are not production-ready; the fight is about the default after 2027 (F8, F9).
  • EIP-8288: the protocol verifies one proof type and stops adding precompiles per algorithm; today it supports only leanSPHINCS and it is not in Hegotá (F11, F12, F13).
  • For Mantle: keep the near-term plan, but change its positioning, address range and timeline, and abstract the verification backend; PQ signature aggregation does not pay off yet (F14, F15, F16).
  • Where to read: basics in I–K, the two paths in L–O, EIP-8288 in P–Q, revisions and Mantle in R–S.

Context

The problem. On 2026-10-16, a16z released Aerie. It aggregates Falcon signatures with a lattice SNARK, about 29× faster than Ethereum's leanVM on one machine. a16z also openly made "the case for lattices" [1]. Two days later, StarkWare co-founder Eli Ben-Sasson replied "Strong disagree" [19]. A day later, Vitalik called EIP-8288 the ideal route to support everyone's cryptography: "Ethereum can then never add a new precompile again" [14].

Why now.

  • Parts 1 and 2 gave the threat inventory and a near-term engineering plan. Part 2 recommends an ML-DSA-65 verification precompile [48]. This debate decides whether that recommendation still holds.
  • In 2026-09, the EF rated the ML-DSA precompile EIP-8355 "premature standardization". On 10-19, EIP-8081 moved it to Declined [36][37].
  • leanVM changed its signature scheme on 10-17, and its benchmark numbers changed with it [6][7]. Without primary sources, second-hand numbers are easy to mix up.

Scope. Principles and trade-offs of both signature families; how the two aggregation paths work and how to read their numbers; each ecosystem's position and reasons; how EIP-8288 works and where it stands; revisions to parts 1 and 2.

Non-goals. No security proofs for lattices or hashes; no independent reproduction of Aerie or leanVM benchmarks; no investment view; nothing about Mantle's non-public architecture.

Decision criteria (set before the results). We compare the two paths on six points:

  1. Security assumption: how young it is and how much structure it has;
  2. Bytes: signatures and aggregates;
  3. Time and memory: proving and verifying;
  4. Signer operational difficulty;
  5. Standardization;
  6. Production readiness.

Gaps in the existing material. The external research (ChatGPT Deep Research) has the right frame [49], but it could not fetch the tweets. It also missed three key changes: leanVM moved to two-level leanXMSS on 10-17; the EF declined 8355; and 8288 gas totals about 33,000, not 3,000. The correction list is in panel U.

I

Background

B-0

Signature aggregation

~3min
What it is

Compress many signatures into one small object, so a verifier checks once. BLS adds signatures directly using elliptic-curve algebra, and the result is 96 bytes.

Why it matters here

No known PQ signature adds up that way [1]. So "aggregation" becomes "produce a proof that every signature in the batch verifies". That is where the two paths compete.

Minimal example

32,768 BLS votes → one 96 B signature. 32,768 Falcon-512 signatures sent one by one take about 21 MB; after Aerie aggregation, about 1.3 MB with salts [3].

Common misconception

Myth: PQ aggregation is just a new byte format. Fact: it turns a cheap addition into proving work that needs dedicated compute [10].

Go deeper

[1] a16z Aerie post, first two paragraphs

B-1

Hash-based signatures: reveal secrets to prove authorization

~4min
What it is

The signer makes a set of secret values and publishes their hashes. To sign, it reveals some secrets chosen by the message; the verifier hashes them again and compares with the public key. XMSS hangs many "one-time keys" under one Merkle root, and the root is the public key.

Why it matters here

Its security depends only on the hash function [31]. That is the "fewest dependencies" Ethereum wants. The cost is that a signature carries a one-time signature plus a Merkle path, so it is large.

Minimal example

leanXMSS: two XMSS levels of height 16, 32 B public key, 2,404 B signature, 201 BLAKE2s compressions to verify [7][8].

Common misconception

Myth: hash-based signatures mean SLH-DSA. Fact: the lean schemes use custom parameters for Ethereum, not NIST parameter sets [6].

Go deeper

[8] leanXMSS spec §2–§5

B-2

Stateful vs stateless

~4min
What it is

XMSS is stateful: each one-time key may sign only one message, and the signer must remember which index it used. SLH-DSA / leanSPHINCS are stateless: larger signatures and more hashing buy freedom from state.

Why it matters here

If a stateful signer uses one index for two different messages, an attacker can forge signatures [31]. Consensus signs once per slot, so the slot number can serve as the state. Multi-device wallets and backup restores cannot do that, so accounts use stateless schemes [1][11].

Minimal example

leanXMSS 2,404 B (stateful, consensus); leanSPHINCS 4,276 B (stateless, accounts and 8288) [8].

Common misconception

Myth: the transaction nonce prevents reuse. Fact: the nonce stops replay; once signature material leaves the device, the index is spent even if the transaction never lands.

Go deeper

[31] NIST SP 800-208 §1.2

B-3

Lattice signatures: find an answer that is correct and short

~5min
What it is

A lattice is a regular grid of points in many dimensions. Finding a short enough vector in certain lattices is slow for all known algorithms. Falcon's private key is a "trapdoor" that lets the signer find a short solution to an equation; the verifier only checks the equation and the length.

Why it matters here

Falcon verification is almost all algebra, with only a handful of hashes [1]. That makes it a good fit for an algebraic proof system such as Aerie.

Minimal example

Falcon verification: s1+h⋅s2≡c(modq) and ‖(s1,s2)‖2≤β2, where c is a hash of the salt and the message [3].

Common misconception

Myth: lattice signatures mean Falcon. Fact: ML-DSA is also a lattice signature and is already standard FIPS 204; Falcon's FN-DSA has no draft yet [29][33].

Go deeper

[3] Aerie specs/falcon.md §2

B-4

Using a proof to "aggregate" signatures

~5min
What it is

The aggregator runs a "verify each signature" program inside a proof system and outputs one proof that the whole batch passed. Other nodes verify only that proof. Recursion takes several proofs as input and proves that they are all valid, producing one new proof.

Why it matters here

This is where the two paths differ. Verifying a hash-based signature takes hundreds of hashes, and proving one hash costs about two orders of magnitude more than computing it [1][6]. So leanVM splits work across machines and merges recursively.

Minimal example

leanVM 2-to-1 recursion: 0.29 s, 201.5 KiB output [6].

Common misconception

Myth: SNARK means an elliptic-curve proof. Fact: SNARK only means "succinct and non-interactive"; Aerie is a lattice SNARK, and a STARK is hash-based.

Go deeper

[2] Aerie paper §1.2

B-5

Frame transactions (EIP-8141)

~l2
What it is

Split one transaction into "frames": verification, payment and execution. The account writes its own verification logic and can authorize with any signature scheme.

Why it matters here

EIP-8288 adds a new frame type (mode 4) to 8141. Hegotá includes 8141, and ethereum.org expects Hegotá on mainnet in 2027 Q2 [12][36][38].

How the two signatures work

Hash-based and lattice signatures both resist quantum attacks, but they "trust" different things. Figure 1 puts their verification steps side by side.

F2

Hash-based signatures trust only the hash, but pay for state and size

confidence: high
Claim

Stateful hash-based signatures depend only on preimage-type hardness of the hash, which quantum computers are not expected to break; but signing two different messages with one index enables forgery. A leanXMSS signature is 2,404 B, about 25× BLS; stateless leanSPHINCS is 4,276 B and SLH-DSA-128s is 7,856 B.

Evidence

observed NIST SP 800-208 §1, §1.2 [31] (H1, H3); leanXMSS and leanSPHINCS specs [8] (H4, H10); FIPS 205 Table 2 [30] (H11).

Implication

Consensus can use the slot as state; accounts must use a stateless scheme, which doubles the signature size again. The "conservative" hash path has a size cost.

F6

Falcon is small and fast; the hard parts are the signer and the standard

confidence: high
Claim

Falcon-512 has an 897 B public key and a 666 B signature, and verifies in about 16 µs on one core. Its signer depends on floating-point Gaussian sampling, with platform, side-channel and memory problems. On 2026-09-28, NIST said FN-DSA will require fixed-point arithmetic and that the number of approved signing procedures is "0"; as of 10-20, FIPS 206 has no public draft.

Evidence

observed Aerie spec parameter table [3] (L4); Aerie paper §6.3 [2] (L6); ePrint 2026/1531, 2026/2313 [44] (L7, L9); NIST pqc-forum [32] (L10, L11); NIST publication list [33] (L12). inferred FN-DSA's final encoding may still change (L13).

Implication

Projects that pick Falcon today use a "pre-standard" version. Algorand uses a deterministic variant that differs from NIST's announced direction for FN-DSA (F10).

ML-DSA is also a lattice signature, but with a different construction: Fiat–Shamir with aborts, based on Module-LWE and SelfTargetMSIS [29] (L2). It is a standard and has HSM support. But its signatures are large (3,309 B for ML-DSA-65). Its hash also depends on the signature itself, so Aerie's trick of "keeping the hash outside the proof" does not apply [1] (L3, L37).

Running example: 32,768 signatures in one slot

Both paths face the same task: about 32,768 validator signatures in one slot, and the whole network must accept them as valid within seconds. a16z says one Aerie machine can handle "roughly an Ethereum slot's worth of signatures" [1] (S2).

Approach Who does it Output Main cost
BLS (today) Any node adds them 96 B Not quantum-safe
leanXMSS + leanVM Several aggregators each prove a slice, then merge recursively About 200–320 KiB per proof Proving hashes is costly; needs several high-spec machines and rounds of recursion
Falcon + Aerie One machine, one proof 1.3 MB with salts; 66 KiB estimated with a common salt One node must collect all signatures; it cannot merge others' proofs

Neither approach removes the computation. Each one only moves where it runs, how the proof travels, and how many nodes repeat the work (S3).

F3

leanXMSS + leanVM is a working prototype, but its scheme and hash still change

confidence: high
Claim

EIP-8292 makes aggregators an opt-in, high-spec role with no stake threshold; leaf and recursive aggregation belong to aggregators, block-level folding to proposers. Targets: typical proofs of 185–230 KiB, and 1,000 signatures in about 2.6 s on 16 cores. leanVM now uses a binary field + WHIR, BLAKE2s is only a placeholder hash, and it says it is "not (yet) production ready". On 10-17 the scheme moved to two-level leanXMSS, and README throughput fell from 1,651 to 1,362 per second.

Evidence

observed EIP-8292 [10] (H28, H29, H30); leanVM README @127510d1 and @3a37fb6 [6] (H17, H19, H21, H22); PR #640 [7] (H4). inferred The two throughput figures measure different signature schemes, so do not mix them (H23).

Implication

Any "leanVM signatures per second" figure needs a commit hash. With the hash not chosen, signatures, proofs and 8288's DEPS_HASH will all still change.

F4

Recursion is the strength of the hash path, and its most attacked point

confidence: medium
Claim

Recursion lets leanVM split work across machines and merge other provers' proofs. a16z argues that each recursive step proves statements about "the hash used for Fiat–Shamir", so the composed security "can't be established even in the random oracle model". Academic work agrees that recursive STARK security analysis is only heuristic. On the other side, leanVM claims 128-bit ROM (64-bit QROM) soundness, while EIP-8292 says about 124 / 62 bits. As of 10-20, we found no direct EF reply to this criticism.

Evidence

observed a16z post [1] (H54); Chiesa and co-authors, ePrint 2023/1646 [41] (H55); leanVM README [6] (H19); EIP-8292 security section [10] (H20); tweet and forum search (H57). inferred This is a gap in the proof model, not an attack on leanVM (L41).

Implication

"The hash path is more conservative" holds for the primitive. The full system also adds assumptions about recursive composition.

F5

The hash path has its own standardization and hardware problems

confidence: medium
Claim

leanXMSS / leanSPHINCS are not NIST parameter sets, leanVM has no final hash, and the consensus spec leanSpec still encodes XMSS over KoalaBear. A hardware-wallet-friendly SPHINCS- variant takes 47.5–390 seconds to sign on a Ledger. An early "round-skipping" attack eroded Poseidon2's security margin, and Ethereum has moved to a binary-field design that needs no circuit-friendly hash.

Evidence

observed leanVM README [6] (H17); leanSpec commit 0b7d33e (H26); SPHINCS- post [43] (H14); Poseidon Initiative slides and ePrint 2026/1692 [40] (H59, H60). inferred "Small-field Poseidon is broken" should narrow to "reduced-round attacks + eroded margin" (H61).

Implication

a16z's point that the hash path is "non-standard, hash undecided" is factual; but the same criticism partly applies to Falcon (F7).

M

Falcon + Aerie: how a16z aggregates lattice signatures

In one sentence: Aerie is a lattice SNARK that proves only one thing: "every Falcon signature in this batch is valid". The verifier computes the hashes itself, and the proof covers only the algebra.

Figure 3 compares what each path puts inside the proof.

How it works [2][3] (L15–L17):

  1. The input is N records of (public key, message, 40 B salt), with N up to 65,536. One proof cannot mix Falcon-512 and Falcon-1024.
  2. The verifier uses each salt and message to compute the target ci itself. This "public salt" idea comes from the 2024 LaBRADOR aggregation work by Aardal and co-authors; Aerie did not invent it.
  3. The prover commits to the signature data with Akita, a lattice polynomial commitment based on Module-SIS.
  4. Three sum-check stages reduce the equation, norm and encoding constraints to one batched opening.
  5. Canonical encoding is also proven. Earlier schemes skipped this and could accept signatures that a standard verifier rejects.
  6. There is no recursion.

How to read the numbers (Apple M4 Max, 16 threads, no GPU, median of 7 runs, signing not included; sizes include salts but not the verifier's own public keys and messages) [2][3] (L19–L23, L26, L27):

Reading Number Caveat

Aggregate size 1,346 KiB (about 42 B per signature, 40 B of it salt) Not constant-size by default
"66 KiB" Estimate for common-salt mode Needs an extra non-standard assumption; no such signer ships
Verify 53.6 ms single thread, 11.6 ms on 16 threads 10× / 3.7× faster than one by one; 70% of the time is hashing

F7

Aerie is fast, but read "66 KiB" and "29×" with their conditions

confidence: high
Claim

Aerie proves 32,768 Falcon-512 signatures in 0.645 s on an M4 Max. The default aggregate is 1,346 KiB because each signature carries a 40 B salt. 66 KiB is an estimate for common-salt mode; no reduction of that mode's unforgeability to a standard lattice assumption is known. 29× is same-machine all-core throughput, and leanVM proof verification is actually faster. Aerie proves security only in the classical ROM, and nobody can merge two Aerie proofs.

Evidence

observed Aerie README and benchmark table [3] (L19, L20, L21, L32); paper Tables 2–5 and §7 [2] (L22, L26, L27, L36); spec §10.1 [3] (L30). The three sources give different absolute throughput numbers (L29).

Implication

Compare the paths end to end: collect, prove, propagate and verify. Also count total machine resources and recovery after failure, not only peak throughput.

F8

Both aggregation stacks say they are not production-ready

confidence: high
Claim

The Aerie README says "research release… Neither parameter set has passed the production acceptance criteria". It does not claim compatibility with the final FIPS 206. The leanVM README says "not (yet) production ready", and formal verification is in progress. The Aerie paper also discloses heavy use of AI models for the implementation, benchmarks and parts of the security proofs.

Evidence

observed Aerie README Status [3] (L35); Aerie paper, AI Assistance section [2] (L39); leanVM README [6] (H19).

Implication

This debate sets the default for after 2027. No project should treat either aggregation stack as a ready dependency today.

Ethereum's hash preference is a protocol preference, not its research scope

confidence: high
Claim

Vitalik and Drake give two public reasons: minimal protocol dependencies, and the risk that AI breaks structured math first. strawmap lists a "hash-based PQ L1" as one of five north stars. But pq.ethereum.org (06-27) still lists Falcon and Dilithium as execution-layer candidates, and PSE has benchmarked Falcon + LaBRADOR aggregation. Vitalik himself accepts that public-key encryption cannot use hashes alone.

Evidence

observed Vitalik tweet and Magicians reply [16][13] (H37, H38, H39); Drake tweet [18] (H40); strawmap [45] (H41); pq.ethereum.org [46] (H43); PSE post [42] (H45). inferred EF texts are not publicly reconciled with each other (H44).

Implication

Do not read Ethereum's position as "lattices are unsafe". Its core aim is "less cryptography in the protocol", which is the same idea behind EIP-8288.

F10

Starknet is "hash-based proofs, free choice for accounts"; Algorand is the one major chain live on lattices

confidence: high
Claim

StarkWare says STARK commitments rely only on hashes. S2morrow and OpenZeppelin have both published Falcon-512 account code (the OZ code has no audit), and StarkWare's roadmap mentions Falcon-512 for on-chain consensus signatures. Algorand's native Falcon-1024 accounts shipped with v5.0.0 (released 2026-08-12); they use a deterministic variant and leave consensus participation keys unchanged. Solana's Alpenglow upgrade uses BLS for consensus and is scheduled for mainnet activation on 2026-10-21; its Falcon code remains prototypes.

Evidence

observed Two StarkWare posts [22][23] (H50, H51); OZ repository [24] (H53); Algorand docs and release [25][27] (L48, L49, L50); two Solana pages [28] (L53, L54). inferred Starknet is not anti-Falcon (H52). speculative Eli's "module-SIS" point targets lattice succinct proofs (Akita) rather than NTRU-based Falcon signatures (H49).

Implication

"Lattice signatures, hash-based proofs" is a combination that already runs. Mantle does not need to join one camp.

a16z's four arguments, one by one:

  1. Performance and simplicity: one machine proves the whole batch with no recursion. If an aggregator fails, any node with the same signatures can redo the proof [1] (S9). Check: true, but it assumes one node first collects every signature; the paper does not model how signatures reach that node (S9).
  2. Recursive security: see F4. It criticizes the proof model; it is not an attack (L41).
  3. Non-standard: true, the lean schemes do not use NIST parameter sets [6] (L43). But FN-DSA has no draft and Aerie is a research release, so this point partly applies to Falcon too (L44).
  4. Privacy: no black-box construction turns hashes into public-key encryption, so private chains will need lattices (ML-KEM) [1] (L45). Vitalik's answer is to keep encrypted notes off-chain [16] (L46).

How real is the AI risk? AI-assisted attacks on lattice-family schemes did happen in 2026: Anthropic used a model to improve HAWK key recovery. The HAWK team withdrew it on 07-29 [34] (L57, L58). But HAWK rests on the lattice isomorphism problem, not on SIS / LWE / NTRU, and Anthropic said the attack does not affect lattice cryptography in general. As of 10-20, this research found no public AI attack on Falcon or ML-DSA themselves (L59, speculative).

F1

The debate is three layered choices, and they can be mixed

confidence: high
Claim

The signature layer (hash / lattice), the aggregation proof layer (hash-based STARK / lattice SNARK) and the protocol interface layer (precompile / EIP-8288) are independent choices. Starknet already runs "hash-based proofs + Falcon accounts". EIP-8288 follows the design "the protocol verifies one hash-based proof type, and accounts can wrap any signature in a STARK".

Evidence

observed StarkWare [22] (H50); EIP-8288 text [11] (E5); Vitalik's 2026-09-09 tweet [17] (E29). inferred The three-layer split and the mixing it allows (S1).

Implication

Before asking "which path", state which layer you are choosing. For Mantle, the account-layer signature choice and the L2 proof system choice are separate decisions.

P

EIP-8288: turning signature checks into "transaction dependencies"

What it is. EIP-8288 (Draft; authors Vitalik and Thomas Coratger; created 2026-06-03; last changed 10-15) adds a new frame type to EIP-8141: the mode 4 "dependency verification frame" [11] (E1–E3).

  • The frame holds 1–256 triples of 96 bytes each: (scheme, data_hash, verification_key_hash).
  • The frame runs no EVM code. It only declares "the block must contain a proof that these dependencies hold".
  • The block header gains a recursive_stark field: one recursive STARK that proves every dependency in the block.
  • The account's verification frame reads the triples with FRAMEPARAM / FRAMEDATACOPY.
  • The account itself checks two things: is the key my authorized key, and is the signed hash this transaction.

Figure 4 walks Alice's transfer to Bob through it.

Cost. Each leanSPHINCS dependency: 3,000 gas of frame execution plus 30,000 added to intrinsic gas, about 33,000 gas in total. The payer pays it even if the transaction reverts [11] (E13, E14, E15). On-chain data is 96 B per dependency, plus one STARK of about 100–300 kB per block [17] (E30).

F11

The core of 8288: the protocol verifies one proof type; accounts keep the algorithms

confidence: high
Claim

On 10-19 Vitalik wrote that the ideal route is "through 8288": "make whatever signature you want locally, then STARK it". He added: "Ethereum can then never add a new precompile again". On Magicians he gave the motive: a "purely hash-based" base layer and less infrastructure to maintain. The spec gives other schemes two options. They go on-chain in the clear and pay gas, or the client wraps them in a STARK for the leanSTARK route.

Evidence

observed Vitalik tweets [14][15] (E26, E27, H42); Magicians reply #5 [13] (E28); EIP-8288 Rationale [11]; Vitalik's 2026-09-09 tweet [17] (E29).

Implication

8288 is not one more account abstraction proposal. It answers "how many kinds of cryptography should the protocol support natively", and its answer is one.

F12

The current draft supports only leanSPHINCS; "just STARK any signature" does not work yet

confidence: high
Claim

Dependency frames accept only leanSPHINCS (0x10) today; leanSTARK (0x11) is a reserved value, and a frame that uses it stays invalid until the spec defines get_deps_hash for it. AGGREGATED_VK, DEPS_HASH and the reference implementation are all TBD. The spec's gas section still describes a KoalaBear field, while leanVM has moved to a binary field. There is no public benchmark for producing a STARK of Falcon / ML-DSA verification on a phone or wallet.

Evidence

observed EIP-8288 @72ca2dd [11] (E5, E8); leanVM README [6] (E50). inferred Spec and implementation disagree (E23); "pick any signature, then STARK it" is not usable yet (E31). speculative Feasibility of client-side STARK generation (E34).

Implication

Vitalik describes the end-state direction, not today's capability. The only signatures 8288 can natively aggregate today are hash-based.

The EF first rated the ML-DSA precompile EIP-8355 tier C, "premature standardization"; on 10-19, EIP-8081 listed it as Declined. 7885 / 8051 / 8052 and 8288 appear in no list. F15

Accounts should target a "verification backend", not one precompile address

confidence: medium
Claim

If an account's verification logic hard-codes a precompile address, any change of algorithm, address or a move to dependency introspection forces an account migration. If the account only knows a "verification backend" interface, the backend can be a precompile or in-SP1 verification, and later an 8288-style dependency check. But 8288 works only for 8141 frame transactions; ERC-4337 accounts cannot declare dependency frames.

Evidence

observed 8288 exposes dependencies through 8141's FRAMEPARAM / FRAMEDATACOPY [11][12] (E20). inferred Verification-backend abstraction (E48); an account that can register keys of several algorithms can switch between ML-DSA and leanSPHINCS without migrating (S7).

Implication

Widen part 2's PQVerifierAdapterV1 from "hide the address" to "hide the backend", and let one account register keys of several algorithms.

F16

PQ signature aggregation does not pay off on Mantle in the near term

confidence: medium
Claim

The spec notes that with leanSPHINCS only, plain concatenation saves space up to about 100 signatures. One leanVM proof is about 320 KiB. On a single-sequencer L2, 8288's multi-hop recursion collapses into one proof by the sequencer, and proving time adds straight to block latency.

Evidence

observed EIP-8288 mempool section [11], leanVM benchmarks [6], Falcon aggregation break-even study [42] (E18, E33, E59). inferred Break-even near 100 signatures, single-sequencer collapse, early adoption not realistic (E51, E52, E58). speculative No public data on the cost of verifying a leanVM STARK inside SP1; it may become a bottleneck (E54).

Implication

In the near term, spend the budget on account and permission paths, not on aggregation. Revisit aggregation when PQ transaction volume and the L1 design take shape.

Suggested actions (inference):

  1. Keep the near-term plan: contract account + PQ verification as the disaster-recovery path for high-value assets. Treat the precompile as transitional (R1).
  2. Fix part 2's three facts: 8355 message format, address range, AA timeline (R2–R4). Check the RIP registry or use the RIP process before picking an address (E45).
  3. Abstract the verification backend and allow several keys: let an account register both an ML-DSA key and a leanSPHINCS key (F15, S7).
  4. Run a separate Falcon test track: measure Falcon / ML-DSA verification cost in op-geth, Kona and SP1. Aerie matters only if Mantle needs batch aggregation (S6).
  5. Do not build PQ signature aggregation until PQ volume and the L1 design take shape (F16).
  6. Track five signals: 8141 activation in Hegotá; whether 8288 enters I* and leanSTARK gets defined; the FN-DSA draft; leanVM's hash choice; a16z's promised "full case" and any EF reply (S8).
CAUTION

Do not switch a whole chain's signature choice because of one benchmark. Aerie and leanVM both say they are not production-ready, and key numbers on both sides changed within one week (F3, F8).
